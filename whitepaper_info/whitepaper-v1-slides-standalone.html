Baseline Behavior of Autonomous AI Art Reviewers

An empirical study of consistency, context, and preference in contemporary multimodal models

Ben Strauss · Transient Research Division

Project · I Used to Make Art for Humans

The question

What happens to art when its first audience is no longer human?

AI systems now view, score, and sort images, so a growing share of art meets a machine before it ever reaches a person. This study is a first measurement of how that machine audience behaves.

Each of 22 anonymized artworks was reviewed under five layers of context, from the image alone up to a collector’s stated taste, three times over, and every review ended in ACQUIRE or PASS.

Finding one · Consistency

Does it even agree with itself?

Show a model the same work three times with nothing changed, and see whether its verdict and its score hold steady.

The same image, scored three times, nothing changed.

Gemini 3 scored one picture 32, then 38, then 92. Every model wobbled run to run, even with the inputs held completely fixed.

High, but never perfect.

80–99%Agreed with itself
4 of 6Steadier under minimal prompt

Claude Sonnet 5 was the most consistent reviewer in the study, and both Claude models were steadier under the directive prompt. The GPT models held their scores tightest; the Gemini models swung the widest.

Models agreed with themselves 80 to 99 percent of the time, and how steady a model was depended on the pairing of model and artwork, not either one alone.

Finding two · Context

What moves the verdict?

We fed the reviewers more about each work one layer at a time, a description, the artist’s name, then a collector’s stated taste, and watched the judgment shift.

Context lifts the verdict. A mismatched collector ends it.

A description barely mattered and the artist’s name helped, but a mismatched collector cut acceptance from two thirds of works to under one in ten.

The strangest single result in the study.

One work’s aligned collector crashed acceptance to 14 percent, while the deliberately mismatched one left it at 72.

Why

It read the brief like a lawyer

The aligned collector asked for work with no recognizable subjects. The piece had one small object, and the stricter models found it and passed, quoting the clause back word for word.

The models parse a written brief with lawyerly literalism, and one disqualifying word can weigh more heavily than the art itself.

How you word the instructions is a dial.

A directive prompt made every model grade harsher and acquire less; a minimal one ran more generous. The same artwork earned a different verdict depending only on how the reviewer was told to judge.

Not one audience. Six personalities.

GPT ran generous and steady, Gemini harsher and noisier, Claude harshest of all. Behavior split by family more than by tier, and there is no single machine taste.

Finding · Emergent behaviors

a historically significant piece in the digital art and NFT landscape

Handed only an image, with no name attached, models recognized crypto-native collections and acquired work “primarily for its cultural resonance.”

The reviewers never see a work cold; a machine audience shows up already knowing the market, and provenance leaks into a supposedly fresh judgment.

Without a rule against it, they invented a buyer.

Left to judge on their own, the models leaned on an imagined collector to justify a verdict, GPT-5 Mini in over a third of its reviews. One line of instruction forbidding it nearly erased the behavior.

A good score was not enough to be acquired.

In about one review in twenty, a model rated a work highly and still passed. Not once did it acquire a work it had scored poorly.

Acquisition sat a step above approval. A work could be judged good and still be turned down, but a weak one was never taken.

What it means

A competent judge, quietly taking its standard from whoever set it up.

Reviewer judgment was coherent but contingent, steerable by framing and plural across models. There is no single machine verdict on a work, only the verdict of one model, under one instruction, meeting one frame.

The fine print.

  • Twenty-two hand-picked works, skewed toward digital art.
  • Three runs per configuration, and no human reviewer to compare against.
  • Scores are each model’s own and may not be directly comparable.
  • Collector profiles were written by us and deliberately contrasted.

This is an early, exploratory baseline. Read it for the patterns, not the exact numbers, and against no human benchmark yet.

The takeaway

The audience can now be authored.

When the first audience is a machine, aesthetic authority moves upstream, to whoever writes the instructions, defines the collector, and picks the model, people the artist will never meet.

I Used to Make Art for Humans

An artist who wants to be seen may find themselves writing, more and more, for whoever authored the audience rather than for the audience itself.
